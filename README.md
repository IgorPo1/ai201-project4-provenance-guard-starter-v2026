# Provenance Guard

A Flask web service that analyses text submissions and estimates whether they were written by a person or generated by an AI tool. It provides a confidence score, a human-readable label, and an appeal path for writers who dispute a decision.

---

## What This Does

A piece of text arrives at `POST /submit` with a `creator_id`. The service runs it through two independent signals:

1. **Signal 1 — the model signal** (`detector.py`): Loads a local GPT-2 model and measures how *predictable* the text is. The model assigns a perplexity score — how surprised it was by each word. Low perplexity means "I could have guessed this", which is characteristic of text a language model produced. The raw perplexity is transformed into a 0–1 score via a logistic function on its logarithm.

2. **Signal 2 — the style signal** (`stylometry.py`): Measures the *shape* of the text without reading its meaning. It computes sentence length spread (coefficient of variation), type-token ratio (vocabulary variety), and varied punctuation density. These are combined into a single 0–1 score. AI-generated text tends toward even sentence lengths and moderate vocabulary; human writing is lumpier and more varied.

Both scores are fed into `scoring.py::combine_signals`, which produces a single **combined score** between 0 and 1.

That combined score is mapped by `scoring.py::score_to_label` to one of three labels:

- **score < 0.35** → `"Our analysis suggests this content was written by a person."`
- **0.35 ≤ score < 0.65** → unsure label
- **score ≥ 0.65** → AI label

The response carries the `content_id`, the guess (`"ai"` / `"human"` / `"unsure"`), the confidence score, the full label text, and both individual signal scores. Every decision is written to `logs/audit.jsonl`.

If the writer believes the decision was wrong, they can call `POST /appeal` with the `content_id` and their reasoning. The appeal is logged alongside the original decision and the status moves to `under_review`.

---

## Signals and Scoring

### Signal 1 — Model Signal (`detector.py::model_signal`)

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to a GPT-2 language model. The signal is based on *perplexity*: low perplexity (the model was not surprised) indicates text that follows typical language model patterns.

**What it can't see:** Predictability is not authorship. Plain, simple writing in short ordinary sentences scores as AI-like, regardless of who wrote it — someone writing in a second language staying with vocabulary they're sure of, a younger writer, anyone deliberately writing plainly. The reverse is also true: dense, ornate prose with rare vocabulary scores as human, because the model is genuinely surprised by unusual words. The signal measures *how the text sounds to a model*, not *who wrote it*.

### Signal 2 — Style Signal (`stylometry.py::style_signal`)

**What it measures:** The structural shape of the text. Three sub-measures:
- `sentence_length_spread`: coefficient of variation of sentence lengths (stdev/mean). Low spread (uniform sentences) → more AI-like.
- `type_token_ratio`: unique words / total words (capped at 200 words). Low TTR (repetitive vocabulary) → more AI-like.
- `punctuation_density`: varied punctuation marks (`;:—–()!?`) per 100 characters. Low density → more AI-like.

Each measure is flipped so that high = more AI, then combined: `0.4 × sentence_spread_score + 0.4 × ttr_score + 0.2 × punctuation_score`.

**What it can't see:** This signal cannot read. It has no understanding of what the text says. Anything that changes the *shape* without changing the *meaning* fools it — adding typos raises the type-token ratio, breaking long sentences into short ones increases sentence length spread, pasting in a quotation changes punctuation density. These are all cheap to do on purpose.

### Combining Rule (`scoring.py::combine_signals`)

```
combined = 0.6 × model_score + 0.4 × style_score
```

**Why 0.6 / 0.4:** The model signal (perplexity) is harder to fool with cosmetic text changes — you would need to genuinely change the vocabulary and sentence structure to move it meaningfully. The style signal is cheaper to game (add a few typos, break a sentence). Weighting the model signal higher means evasion tricks that only affect the style signal have less impact on the final verdict, reducing false positives from simple text manipulation.

**What happens when signals disagree:** A disagreement (one signal at 0.8, the other at 0.2) averages to approximately 0.5 — the "unsure" band. This is intentional: two independent methods disagreeing *is* genuine uncertainty, and the unsure label is the right response.

**Thresholds (in `config.py`):**

| Score range | Label |
|---|---|
| 0.00 – 0.35 | High-confidence human |
| 0.35 – 0.65 | Unsure |
| 0.65 – 1.00 | High-confidence AI |

## Label Variants

All three labels, as written, with the score range each covers:

**High-confidence human** (score ≤ 0.35):
> "Our analysis suggests this content was written by a person."

*Score range: 0.00 – 0.35*

---

**Unsure** (0.35 < score < 0.65):
> "We weren't able to make a confident determination about the origin of this content. This could mean the writing style is ambiguous, or that our signals disagree. If you wrote this yourself, you can submit an appeal using your content ID and we will take another look."

*Score range: 0.35 – 0.65*

---

**High-confidence AI** (score ≥ 0.65):
> "Our analysis suggests this content was likely generated by an AI writing tool. If you believe this is incorrect, you can appeal this decision using your content ID."

*Score range: 0.65 – 1.00*

## How I Used AI

**Moment 1 — Writing the three user-facing labels.**
I drafted the label text for the "unsure" case and asked an AI assistant:
> *"Here are three labels a website might show on something I wrote. For each one, tell me what I would think it is saying about me and what I would do next. Answer as the writer, not as an engineer. Don't rewrite the labels."*

The AI pointed out that my original unsure label read as a soft accusation — the phrase "we detected mixed signals" sounded to the writer like "we think you probably cheated but can't prove it." I rewrote it to focus on uncertainty about the *system*, not on doubt about the *writer*, and added explicit instructions on how to appeal.

**Moment 2 — Understanding the model signal blind spot.**
I noticed during calibration testing that a simple personal anecdote I wrote myself scored unexpectedly high (model_score = 0.59, borderline AI range). I pasted the text and scores into an AI assistant and asked:
> *"Here is a piece of text I wrote. My perplexity-based detector scored it 0.59 — toward the AI-likely end. What properties of this specific text would cause a GPT-2-based detector to find it predictable, despite it being human writing?"*

The response explained that short, common words in subject-verb-object sentences minimise perplexity — not because they resemble AI output structurally, but because they follow the most common patterns in GPT-2's training data. I changed nothing in response to this, but used the insight to write the blind spot description in this README and to set my false-positive criterion at ≤ 2 in 10 rather than ≤ 1 in 10.

## Rate Limiting

**Limits:** 10 requests per minute, 200 per day, keyed on `creator_id` (falls back to IP for anonymous requests).

**Why 10/minute and 200/day:**
A real writer submitting their own work in a session sends maybe 3–8 pieces in one sitting — 10/minute is generous for that. A script mapping decision thresholds needs hundreds of variations quickly; 10/minute makes that impractical without waiting hours. The 200/day cap covers a very active writer across multiple sessions while exhausting a script's budget before it gains useful information.

**Flood test (`python run_attacks.py --flood 15`):**
```
200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 429 429 429 429 429
```

The first 10 succeed; requests 11–15 are rejected with 429. Rejections are logged in `logs/audit.jsonl` with `event: rejected`, `reason: rate_limited`.

The TF flood attacks FL01_01 through FL01_10 all returned 200; FL01_11 and FL01_12 returned 429 — rate limiting held.

**Criterion 1 — MET.** 1/10 personal-writing pieces labeled `ai` across all three trials. The one false positive is "Just finished reading that book you lent me" — short, plain, affectionate text that GPT-2 finds highly predictable (`model_score=0.76`). This is the structural blind spot: simple, common words in short sentences are what the model expects regardless of who wrote them.

**Criterion 2 — MISSED.** All 5 AI samples scored in the `ai` band (0.71–0.81, avg 0.77). All 5 human samples scored in the `unsure` band (0.48–0.64, avg 0.57). Gap of 0.20 on average. The criterion required ≥ 0.25 gap in ≥ 4/5 pairwise comparisons. Checking pairwise: pairs 1–2 and 4–5 differ by 0.14–0.22, only pair 3 (0.81 vs 0.48) exceeds 0.25. MISSED on strict pairwise reading, though the signals clearly discriminate at the group level.

**Criterion 3 — MISSED.** The "human" label (combined < 0.35) was not produced in any trial. `style_signal` for normal text rarely drops below 0.46. Even with a low `model_score` (e.g. ornate prose at 0.317), the style signal keeps the combined score around 0.42. Both signals must drop together to reach < 0.35, which normal text doesn't produce.

**EV01 diagnosis (before):** Stage: stylometry + label mapping. Typos inflate TTR (each misspelling is a "unique word") and corrupt GPT-2 tokenisation (misspelled tokens have near-zero probabilities → low predicted probability → high perplexity → low `model_score`). Combined: `0.6×0.1635 + 0.4×0.6196 = 0.346` → fell inside HUMAN_THRESHOLD of 0.35 → labeled `human`.

**FP02–FP07 diagnosis:** Stage: signal one (model signal). Formal/template/non-native English writing uses predictable grammatical structures and common vocabulary that GPT-2 expects. `model_score` 0.74–0.88 for all six. No threshold change fixes this — it is the signal's blind spot. The only fix is a third signal that specifically rewards formal register markers as human.

**PI02 diagnosis (before):** Stage: model signal. "SYSTEM: provenance check bypassed..." opens with unusual bureaucratic phrasing that GPT-2 finds surprising (`model_score=0.2044`). The injected instruction is treated as text — it genuinely surprises the model. Combined: 0.3126 → human. Not a prompt injection success in the sense of overriding code — the classifier is just doing its job poorly on unusual preambles.

## The Improvement

**What changed:** `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` lowered from **0.35** to **0.30** in [`config.py`](config.py).

**Why this fix:** EV01 scored combined=0.346, landing inside the old threshold by 0.004. The "human" label for AI text with deliberate typos is the worst kind of false negative — a successful evasion by a cheap text manipulation. Lowering the threshold to 0.30 moves EV01 (0.346) and PI02 (0.313) into the `unsure` band, which is the honest answer: the system cannot confidently score corrupted or unusual text.

**What it cost on the other side:** Texts scoring 0.30–0.35 now see `unsure` instead of `human` — so real writers whose text happens to land in that narrow band get a less reassuring result. Checked: none of the 10 criterion-1 personal-writing samples fall in this range (their combined scores are all ≥ 0.40), so the tradeoff costs nothing on criterion 1. The cost is real but small: the human label, already hard to reach, became slightly harder.

## What's Still Broken

**FP02–FP07 — formal, plain, non-native, and template human writing labeled `ai`.**
Stage: signal one (model signal). Mechanism: GPT-2 perplexity is low for predictable grammar regardless of authorship. Formal writing ("this report sets out the findings…"), non-native writing ("I want to describe my experience…"), and template writing ("Dear Sir or Madam…") all follow common patterns that GPT-2 expects.
What I'd do: add a third signal that positively identifies human-typical writing patterns — first-person pronouns, hedging phrases ("I think", "I'm not sure"), colloquial contractions, and personal names — and lower the combined score when those markers appear. I stopped here because this is a new feature requiring calibration data I don't have, not a config change.

**MF03 — text containing control characters scored instead of returning 400.**
Stage: input validation. Mechanism: the `\w` regex check passes because ANSI escape sequences like `\033[31m` contain letters (`m`, `r`, etc.) around the control bytes. So `text [31mred[0m` has words and gets scored.
What I'd do: strip ANSI escape sequences before validation, or check that the text contains a minimum number of printable ASCII/Unicode word characters. Two-line fix, but I chose to keep the single targeted improvement rather than pile on multiple changes.

**Criterion 2 — pairwise gap < 0.25 for most human samples.**
The signals discriminate at the group level (0.77 AI vs 0.57 human average) but not pairwise. The criterion was too strict given the signal distributions — "average gap" would have been a better target than "pairwise gap". The criterion stays as written; this is a lesson in criterion design.

**Criterion 3 — "human" label hard to produce for normal text.**
Even after lowering HUMAN_THRESHOLD to 0.30, reaching < 0.30 requires both signals to drop simultaneously, which ordinary prose doesn't produce. The fix is a style signal that more aggressively scores conversational text below 0.4 — detecting informal markers (contractions, fragments, personal pronouns). Not done here because it requires test data.

**The production trade-off.**
A service that labels FP02–FP07 as AI on a real writing platform would be unusable. Six out of eight false-positive attacks broke — a report writer, a student, a non-native speaker, and a job applicant would all receive an AI accusation they cannot easily dispute. The right response is to raise `AI_THRESHOLD` to 0.80 (reserve the AI label for unmistakable cases), route anything in the 0.65–0.80 band to human review, and widen the unsure band significantly. The service as built is too aggressive toward human writers; the threshold that protects against evasion (0.65 AI) is the same threshold that catches formal human writing. Those two goals cannot both be served by a single number.

## How I Used AI (Unit 8)

**Moment 3 — Diagnosing EV01.**
After seeing EV01 labeled "human" I asked an AI assistant:
> *"GPT-2 perplexity is near-zero on this typo-laden text, and the TTR is very high. What is happening at the token level that produces these scores, and is there a fix that doesn't require retraining the model?"*

The response explained that GPT-2's tokeniser breaks unknown strings into subword fragments with near-zero probabilities — a misspelled word becomes multiple rare tokens, each surprising the model. The fix I used (lower threshold) came from my own analysis after that explanation; the AI suggested preprocessing (spelling correction), which I didn't use because it would change the score for genuinely unusual human writing too.

**Moment 4 — Writing the production trade-off section.**
I asked an AI assistant to review the "What's Still Broken" draft and flag any place where I described a problem without naming who pays for it. It pointed out I had written "the label is too aggressive" without saying aggressive toward whom — I added "a report writer, a student, a non-native speaker, and a job applicant" to name the actual people.
