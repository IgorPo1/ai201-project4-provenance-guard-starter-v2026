# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---

## 1. False positive rate on human writing

Of 10 pieces of text I personally wrote — journal entries, emails, and short
paragraphs — at most 2 are labeled **high-confidence AI** across all three
evaluation trials.

**Why this target:** On a writing site, wrongly accusing a real author of using
AI is the most damaging outcome. It can damage reputation and trust. I allow 2
out of 10 (not 1) because both signals have a structural blind spot: signal one
(perplexity) penalises plain, simple writing regardless of authorship, and some
of my own writing is deliberately plain. I accept that rate rather than widen
the unsure band so far that real AI text always escapes. If any single trial
returns 3 or more false positives, the criterion fails, because a fluke of 2
followed by 3 means the system is not consistently safe.

---

## 2. Score spread between clearly-AI and clearly-human text

Of 5 clearly-AI samples (paragraphs generated by an LLM with no editing) and 5
clearly-human samples (published personal essays, letters, diary entries), the
average AI-sample score is at least **0.25 higher** than the average
human-sample score in at least 4 of 5 paired comparisons, in every trial.

**Why this target:** If the two signals cannot distinguish text that is
obviously at opposite ends of the spectrum, they cannot distinguish anything in
between either. A gap of 0.25 on a 0–1 scale is modest — I am not claiming
high accuracy — but it is enough to prove the signals are pointing in the right
direction. I chose pairwise comparisons (not just averages) because a system
that accidentally pairs the wrong extremes can look better than it is on an
average alone. "4 of 5" leaves room for one edge case (e.g. a ChatGPT paragraph
that uses unusual vocabulary and confuses the perplexity signal), which is
realistic.

---

## 3. All three labels are reachable

When I submit one piece of text I am confident is AI-generated, one I am
confident is human-written, and one I judge genuinely borderline (e.g. a lightly
edited AI paragraph), the service returns each of the three distinct labels —
**high-confidence AI**, **unsure**, and **high-confidence human** — at least
once across those three submissions, in every trial.

**Why this target:** If one label is unreachable, the system is effectively
binary. The "unsure" label is the most important one to verify: it is the label
that protects borderline cases from a wrong accusation, and if the thresholds
are misconfigured it disappears entirely. "At least once across three inputs"
is the minimum bar — I am not claiming perfect placement, only that the label
exists as a live output the service actually returns. Testing this before unit 8
also tells me whether my threshold choices leave a gap.

---

## 4. Appeal path always records and changes status

Of 5 appeals submitted via `POST /appeal` (using real `content_id` values from
prior `/submit` calls), all 5 produce a response with `status: under_review`,
and all 5 are visible in `GET /log` with the `under_review` status and the
writer's reasoning recorded alongside the original decision — in every trial.

**Why this target:** The appeal path is the only recourse a wrongly-accused
writer has. A system that accepts an appeal but does not record it, or records
it without changing the status, offers the appearance of fairness without the
substance. I require all 5 of 5 because this is a deterministic code path with
no randomness — there is no reason it should ever fail on valid input, and
accepting any failure rate here would mean accepting that some writers' appeals
silently disappear.

---

## 5. Bad input returns 400, not 500

When `POST /submit` receives each of the following three malformed requests —
(a) `text` is an empty string, (b) the `text` field is absent entirely, (c)
`text` is `null` — the service returns HTTP status **400** (not 500, not 200,
not any crash) in all 3 of 3 cases, in every trial.

**Why this target:** A 500 on bad input means the service has crashed and
revealed internal state. A 400 means the service understood the request was
invalid and said so cleanly. On a writing site, bad input will arrive — from
broken clients, from network glitches that truncate a payload, from deliberate
probing. I require 3 of 3 (not just "most of the time") because this is input
validation, which is a conditional that either runs or doesn't. A system that
crashes on empty text will crash on an attacker's empty text too, and the
error page that comes back is information about the internals that should not
be shared.
